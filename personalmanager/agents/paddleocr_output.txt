## 1 · CONSTITUTIONAL DEVELOPMENT, 1773-1947

### A. Company Rule, 1773-1858

## Regulating Act, 1773

functions.



Governor of Bengal designated Governor-General of Bengal — first was Warren Hastings; an Executive Council of four members created.



Governors of Bombay and Madras made subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal.

Provided for a Supreme Court at Calcutta (established 1774) — one Chief Justice and three other judges.

Barred Company servants from private trade and from accepting presents or bribes.

Court of Directors to report on revenue, civil and military affairs to the British Government.

## Amending Act of 1781 (Act of Settlement)

Governor-General-in-Council exempted from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction for acts done in official capacity;Company servants likewise.



Revenue matters excluded from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction.

Supreme Court to have jurisdiction over all inhabitants of Calcutta; to apply personal law of the defendant.

•Appeals from Provincial Courts to lie to the Governor-General-in-Council.

## Pitt's India Act, 1784

Separated the Company's commercial and political functions.

Court of Directors for commerce; a new Board of Control for political affairs — the system of Double Government.

Board of Control empowered to supervise and direct all civil, military and revenue operations.

Company's territories called "British possessions in India" for the first time.

## i 

 L ord Cornwallis given power to override his Council; also made Commander-in-Chief.

## Charter Act, 1793

Extended the Company's trade monopoly for 20 more years.

Override power over the Council extended to all future Governors-General and Governors.

Commander-in-Chief not to be a member of the Council unless so appointed.

Board of Control members to be paid out of Indian revenues.

## Charter Act, 1813

Ended the Company's trade monopoly — except for tea and trade with China.

Asserted the sovereignty of the British Crown over Company territories.

Permitted Christian missionaries to preach in India.

One lakh rupees a year set aside for the revival of literature and the encouragement of the sciences among Indians.

## Charter Act, 1833 — the final step of centralisation 

Governor-General of Bengal → Governor-General of India, with all civil and military powers; first was Lord William Bentinck.



•Governments of Bombay and Madras deprived of legislative powers — exclusive legislative power vested in the Governor-General of India.



•Company's commercial activities ended entirely; it became a purely administrative body holding territory "in trust for His Majesty".



•Attempted to introduce open competition for the selection of civil servants — negated by the Court of Directors.

First Act to prohibit disqualification of an Indian on grounds of religion, place of birth, descent or colour.

## Indian Councils Act, 1861 — beginning of representative institutions 

Viceroy to nominate Indians as non-official members of his expanded Council. Lord Canning nominated three in 1862— the Raja of Benaras, the Maharaja of Patiala and Sir Dinkar Rao.



Decentralisation begins — legislative powers restored to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies.

•New legislative councils provided for Bengal (1862), North-Western Provinces (1866) and Punjab (1897).

Gave statutory recognition to the portfolio system introduced by Lord Canning in 1859.

Viceroy empowered to issue ordinances, valid for six months, during an emergency.

## Indian Councils Act, 1892

Increased the number of non-official members in the central and provincial legislative councils.

Councils given the power to discuss the budget and to address questions to the executive.

Nomination of some non-official members on the recommendation of district boards, municipalities, universities,trade associations and chambers of commerce — indirect election in substance, though the word "election" was never used.



## Indian Councils Act, 1909 - Morley-Minto Reforms 

Size of the legislative councils considerably increased — the Central Legislative Council from 16 to 60 members.

Retained the official majority at the Centre, but allowed provincial legislative councils a non-official majority.

Enlarged the deliberative functions — supplementary questions, resolutions on the budget.

For the first time provided for the association of Indians with the executive councils — Satyendra Prasad Sinha became the first Indian to join the Viceroy's Executive Council, as Law Member.



Introduced separate electorates for Muslims — legalised communalism. Lord Minto is called the Father of Communal Electorates.



## Government of India Act, 1919 — Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms 

Separated central and provincial subjects; each authorised to raise its own revenues; provincial and central budgets separated.



Dyarchy in the provinces — provincial subjects split into transferred (administered by the Governor with ministers responsible to the legislature) and reserved (administered by the Governor with his executive council, not responsible to the legislature).



Introduced bicameralism and direct election at the Centre — a Legislative Assembly (lower house) and a Council of State (upper house).



Required three of the six members of the Viceroy's Executive Council, other than the Commander-in-Chief, to be Indian.

Extended communal representation to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans.

•Franchise granted to a limited number on the basis of property, tax or education.

•Created the office of High Commissioner for India in London.

•Provided for a Public Service Commission — set up in 1926.

Provided for a statutory commission after ten years to review its working — the Simon Commission, appointed 1927.

Abolished dyarchy in the provinces and introduced provincial autonomy — provinces became autonomous units administering their own subjects.



Provided for dyarchy at the Centre — never implemented.

Introduced bicameralism in six of the eleven provinces — Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam and the United Provinces.

## Indian Independence Act, 1947

•Ended British rule on 15 August 1947 and created two independent dominions — India and Pakistan — free to leave the Commonwealth.



Abolished the office of the Secretary of State for India.

Governor-General and provincial Governors made constitutional heads, acting on the advice of ministers.

•Granted full legislative sovereignty to the Constituent Assembly, which also functioned as the dominion legislature

•Dropped the title "Emperor of India" from the royal style of the British Crown.

<div style="text-align: center;"><html><body><table border="1"><tr><td>Lengthiest written constitution</td><td>Originally 395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules; presently about 470 Articles, 25 Parts, 12 Schedules. Unitary features: strong Centre, single constitution, single citizenship, integrated judiciary, all-India services, appointment of Governors by the Centre, emergency provisions, flexibility of amendment.</td></tr><tr><td>Parliamentary government</td><td>Nominal and real executives · majority party rule · collective responsibility to the legislature · ministers sitting in the legislature · leadership of the Prime Minister : dissolution of the lower house. The Constitution establishes a parliamentary system at both levels, not the American presidential model.</td></tr><tr><td>Parliamentary sovereignty with judicial supremacy</td><td>A synthesis — the Supreme Court can declare parliamentary laws unconstitutional through judicial review; ## Key terms 

•Sovereign — India is neither a dependency nor a dominion; free in internal and external affairs. Membership of the Commonwealth and the United Nations does not limit sovereignty.



Socialist — added by the 42nd Amendment; democratic socialism, a mixed economy, not the communist variety.Secular — added by the 42nd Amendment; no state religion, equal respect for all faiths.



Democratic — representative, parliamentary democracy resting on universal adult franchise, periodic elections and rule of law; covers social and economic as well as political democracy.

## Effects of a National Emergency 

Executive — the Centre may give executive directions to a state on any matter; the state government is not suspended, but is brought under complete Central control.



Legislative — Parliament may make laws on subjects in the State List. Such laws cease to operate six months after the emergency ends. The state legislature is not suspended.



Financial — the President may modify the distribution of revenues between the Centre and the states; such an order operates till the end of the financial year in which the emergency ceases.



Term of the legislature — the life of the Lok Sabha may be extended by one year at a time, not extending beyond six months after the emergency ceases. The same applies to a state assembly.



•Article 358 — the six rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended. Since the 44th Amendment this operates only when the emergency is declared on the ground of war or external aggression, not armed rebellion, and only for laws related to the emergency.



Article 359 — the President may by order suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of specified Fundamental Rights. Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended — a safeguard introduced by the 44th Amendment.

## Effects of President's Rule 

The President assumes the functions of the state government and the powers of the Governor and of any state authority other than the High Court.



The state legislature is either suspended or dissolved; Parliament passes the state legislative bills and the state budget.



Parliament may delegate the law-making power for the state to the President or another authority.

The President cannot assume the powers of the High Court or suspend any provision relating to it.

## PROCEDURE - POINTS THAT ARE TESTED DIRECTLY 

•An amendment bill may be introduced in either House, and by a private member as well as a minister; no prior recommendation of the President is required.



•It must be passed separately in each House — there is no provision for a joint sitting in case of disagreement.

Where state ratification is needed, the states must ratify before the bill is presented to the President; there is no time limit,and a state cannot withdraw its ratification once given.



•The President must give assent — he can neither withhold assent nor return the bill for reconsideration.

• The Constitution can be amended only by Parliament; state legislatures cannot initiate an amendment, except a resolution for the abolition or creation of a legislative council.

## 12 · THE BASIC STRUCTURE DOCTRINE 

•The question is how far Parliament's amending power under Article 368 extends. The Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) settled it: Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution, including the Fundamental Rights, but cannot alter its basic structure.



The doctrine applies to amendments made on or after 24 April 1973, the date of that judgment.

The Constitution nowhere defines the basic structure. It has been built up case by case, and the list is not closed.

## Elements recognised as part of the basic structure 

Supremacy of the Constitution · sovereign, democratic and republican nature of the polity · secular character · separation of powers between the legislature, executive and judiciary · federal character · unity and integrity of the nation · welfare state and the socio-economic justice objective · judicial review · independence of the judiciary · the rule of law · harmony and balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles · free and fair elections · the principle of equality · limited power of Parliament to amend the Constitution · effective access to justice · powers of the Supreme Court under Articles 32, 136,141 and 142 · parliamentary system of government.



## POINTS ON WHICH THE CURRENT POSITION DIFFERS FROM THE OLDER ONE 

• The right to property is no longer a Fundamental Right. The 44th Amendment, 1978 removed Article 31 and made it a legal right under Article 300A.



• The nomination of Anglo-Indian members to the Lok Sabha and state assemblies ceased with effect from 25 January 2020 under the 104th Amendment.



• The women's reservation introduced by the 106th Amendment, 2023 has been enacted but is not yet in operation — it takes effect only after the next census and the delimitation that follows.
