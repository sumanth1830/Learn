1 · CONSTITUTIONAL DEVELOPMENT, 1773–1947
A. Company Rule, 1773–1858
Regulating Act, 1773
• First step by the British Parliament to regulate the affairs of the East India Company; first recognition of its political
functions.
• Governor of Bengal designated Governor-General of Bengal — first was Warren Hastings; an Executive Council of
four members created.
• Governors of Bombay and Madras made subordinate to the Governor-General of Bengal.
• Provided for a Supreme Court at Calcutta (established 1774) — one Chief Justice and three other judges.
• Barred Company servants from private trade and from accepting presents or bribes.
• Court of Directors to report on revenue, civil and military affairs to the British Government.
Amending Act of 1781 (Act of Settlement)
• Governor-General-in-Council exempted from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction for acts done in official capacity;
Company servants likewise.
• Revenue matters excluded from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction.
• Supreme Court to have jurisdiction over all inhabitants of Calcutta; to apply personal law of the defendant.
• Appeals from Provincial Courts to lie to the Governor-General-in-Council.
Pitt's India Act, 1784
• Separated the Company's commercial and political functions.
• Court of Directors for commerce; a new Board of Control for political affairs — the system of Double Government.
• Board of Control empowered to supervise and direct all civil, military and revenue operations.
• Company's territories called "British possessions in India" for the first time.
Act of 1786
• Lord Cornwallis given power to override his Council; also made Commander-in-Chief.
Charter Act, 1793
• Extended the Company's trade monopoly for 20 more years.
• Override power over the Council extended to all future Governors-General and Governors.
• Commander-in-Chief not to be a member of the Council unless so appointed.
• Board of Control members to be paid out of Indian revenues.
Charter Act, 1813
• Ended the Company's trade monopoly — except for tea and trade with China.
• Asserted the sovereignty of the British Crown over Company territories.
• Permitted Christian missionaries to preach in India.
• One lakh rupees a year set aside for the revival of literature and the encouragement of the sciences among Indians.
Charter Act, 1833 — the final step of centralisation
• Governor-General of Bengal → Governor-General of India, with all civil and military powers; first was Lord William
Bentinck.
• Governments of Bombay and Madras deprived of legislative powers — exclusive legislative power vested in the
Governor-General of India.
• Company's commercial activities ended entirely; it became a purely administrative body holding territory "in trust for
His Majesty".
• Attempted to introduce open competition for the selection of civil servants — negated by the Court of Directors.
• First Act to prohibit disqualification of an Indian on grounds of religion, place of birth, descent or colour.

Charter Act, 1853 — the last of the Charter Acts
• Separated the legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General's Council for the first time.
• Created a separate Indian (Central) Legislative Council with six new legislative members — four appointed by the
local governments of Bengal, Madras, Bombay and Agra: the first, indirect, element of local representation.
• Introduced open competition for the civil service, thrown open to Indians — the Macaulay Committee was appointed
in 1854.
• Company allowed to retain possession without specifying any period — a clear signal that its rule could be ended at
any time.

B. Crown Rule, 1858–1947
Government of India Act, 1858 — the Act for the Good Government of India
• Abolished the East India Company; powers of government, territories and revenues transferred to the British Crown.
• Governor-General of India redesignated Viceroy — first was Lord Canning.
• Abolished the Board of Control and the Court of Directors — ended the system of double government.
• Created the office of Secretary of State for India — a member of the British Cabinet, responsible to Parliament,
assisted by a Council of India of 15 members (advisory).
• Secretary of State-in-Council constituted a body corporate, able to sue and be sued.
Indian Councils Act, 1861 — beginning of representative institutions
• Viceroy to nominate Indians as non-official members of his expanded Council. Lord Canning nominated three in 1862
— the Raja of Benaras, the Maharaja of Patiala and Sir Dinkar Rao.
• Decentralisation begins — legislative powers restored to the Bombay and Madras Presidencies.
• New legislative councils provided for Bengal (1862), North-Western Provinces (1866) and Punjab (1897).
• Gave statutory recognition to the portfolio system introduced by Lord Canning in 1859.
• Viceroy empowered to issue ordinances, valid for six months, during an emergency.
Indian Councils Act, 1892
• Increased the number of non-official members in the central and provincial legislative councils.
• Councils given the power to discuss the budget and to address questions to the executive.
• Nomination of some non-official members on the recommendation of district boards, municipalities, universities,
trade associations and chambers of commerce — indirect election in substance, though the word "election" was
never used.
Indian Councils Act, 1909 — Morley–Minto Reforms
• Size of the legislative councils considerably increased — the Central Legislative Council from 16 to 60 members.
• Retained the official majority at the Centre, but allowed provincial legislative councils a non-official majority.
• Enlarged the deliberative functions — supplementary questions, resolutions on the budget.
• For the first time provided for the association of Indians with the executive councils — Satyendra Prasad Sinha
became the first Indian to join the Viceroy's Executive Council, as Law Member.
• Introduced separate electorates for Muslims — legalised communalism. Lord Minto is called the Father of
Communal Electorates.
Government of India Act, 1919 — Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms
• Separated central and provincial subjects; each authorised to raise its own revenues; provincial and central budgets
separated.
• Dyarchy in the provinces — provincial subjects split into transferred (administered by the Governor with ministers
responsible to the legislature) and reserved (administered by the Governor with his executive council, not responsible to
the legislature).
• Introduced bicameralism and direct election at the Centre — a Legislative Assembly (lower house) and a Council of
State (upper house).
• Required three of the six members of the Viceroy's Executive Council, other than the Commander-in-Chief, to be Indian.
• Extended communal representation to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians and Europeans.
• Franchise granted to a limited number on the basis of property, tax or education.
• Created the office of High Commissioner for India in London.
• Provided for a Public Service Commission — set up in 1926.
• Provided for a statutory commission after ten years to review its working — the Simon Commission, appointed 1927.

Government of India Act, 1935 — the longest Act and the immediate ancestor of the Constitution
• Provided for an All-India Federation of provinces and princely states — it never came into being, as the princely
states did not join.
• Divided powers into three lists — Federal List (59 items), Provincial List (54 items), Concurrent List (36 items);
residuary powers vested in the Viceroy.
• Abolished dyarchy in the provinces and introduced provincial autonomy — provinces became autonomous units
administering their own subjects.
• Provided for dyarchy at the Centre — never implemented.
• Introduced bicameralism in six of the eleven provinces — Bengal, Bombay, Madras, Bihar, Assam and the United
Provinces.
• Extended separate electorates further — to depressed classes, women and labour.
• Abolished the Council of India established by the Act of 1858.
• Provided for a Federal Court — set up in 1937; a Reserve Bank of India — set up in 1935; and a Federal Public
Service Commission, Provincial PSCs and Joint PSCs.
Indian Independence Act, 1947
• Ended British rule on 15 August 1947 and created two independent dominions — India and Pakistan — free to leave
the Commonwealth.
• Abolished the office of the Secretary of State for India.
• Governor-General and provincial Governors made constitutional heads, acting on the advice of ministers.
• Granted full legislative sovereignty to the Constituent Assembly, which also functioned as the dominion legislature.
• Lapse of British paramountcy over the princely states, which were free to accede to either dominion or remain
independent.
• Dropped the title "Emperor of India" from the royal style of the British Crown.

4 · SALIENT FEATURES OF THE CONSTITUTION
Lengthiest written constitution Originally 395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules; presently about 470 Articles, 25
Parts, 12 Schedules. Length owes to the size and diversity of the country, a single
constitution for Centre and states, the influence of the 1935 Act, and the inclusion of
administrative detail.
Drawn from various sources Structural part largely from the Act of 1935; philosophical part from American and Irish
sources; political part from the British model.
Blend of rigidity and flexibility Three amendment routes — simple majority, special majority, and special majority with
state ratification.
Federal system with unitary bias Federal features: two governments, division of powers, written and supreme constitution,
rigidity, independent judiciary, bicameralism. Unitary features: strong Centre, single
constitution, single citizenship, integrated judiciary, all-India services, appointment of
Governors by the Centre, emergency provisions, flexibility of amendment.
Parliamentary government Nominal and real executives · majority party rule · collective responsibility to the
legislature · ministers sitting in the legislature · leadership of the Prime Minister ·
dissolution of the lower house. The Constitution establishes a parliamentary system at
both levels, not the American presidential model.
Parliamentary sovereignty with A synthesis — the Supreme Court can declare parliamentary laws unconstitutional through
judicial supremacy judicial review; Parliament can amend most of the Constitution through its constituent
power.
Integrated and independent A single system with the Supreme Court at the apex, High Courts below and subordinate
judiciary courts under them, enforcing both central and state laws.
Fundamental Rights Justiciable rights in Part III, enforceable through the courts, but not absolute — subject to
reasonable restrictions.
Directive Principles Non-justiciable ideals in Part IV directed at the state, aimed at a welfare state.
Fundamental Duties Added by the 42nd Amendment on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee;
non-justiciable.
Indian secularism No state religion; equal respect for all religions rather than a wall of separation.
Universal adult franchise Every citizen of 18 years and above entitled to vote without any qualification of property,
tax, education or sex.
Single citizenship Only citizenship of India — no separate state citizenship, unlike the United States.
Independent bodies Election Commission, Comptroller and Auditor-General, Union and State Public Service
Commissions, Finance Commission and others, given security of tenure and service
conditions.
Emergency provisions Enable the Centre to meet extraordinary situations; the federal structure converts to a
unitary one without a formal amendment.
Three-tier government Panchayats and municipalities added as a third tier by the 73rd and 74th Amendments.
Cooperative societies Right to form cooperative societies made a Fundamental Right, with a Directive Principle
for their promotion.

7 · THE UNION AND ITS TERRITORY
Article 1 "India, that is Bharat, shall be a Union of States." Territory of India comprises (i) territories of the states,
(ii) Union Territories, and (iii) territories that may be acquired. "Union" was preferred to "Federation"
because the Indian federation is not the result of an agreement among the states, and no state has the
right to secede.
Article 2 Parliament may admit into the Union, or establish, new states on such terms as it thinks fit. This relates to
states not part of India.
Article 3 Parliament may form a new state by separation of territory or by uniting territories; increase or diminish the
area of any state; alter the boundaries or the name of any state. This relates to states already part of
India — the reorganisation of internal territory.
Article 4 Laws made under Articles 2 and 3 are not deemed amendments under Article 368 — they are passed by
simple majority and by the ordinary legislative process.

Reorganisation of states — the sequence
Dhar Commission, 1948 Recommended reorganisation on the basis of administrative convenience rather than
language.
JVP Committee, 1948 Nehru, Patel and Pattabhi Sitaramayya — rejected language as the basis of reorganisation.
Andhra, 1953 The first state created on a linguistic basis, carved out of Madras.
Fazl Ali Commission, 1953 The States Reorganisation Commission — Fazl Ali, K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru. Accepted
language as a basis but rejected "one language one state".
1956 States Reorganisation Act and the 7th Amendment — 14 states and 6 Union
Territories; the four-fold Part A, B, C and D classification abolished.
Present position 28 states and 8 Union Territories.
DISTINCTION WORTH HOLDING
Cession of Indian territory to a foreign state cannot be done under Article 3 — it requires a constitutional amendment
under Article 368. But the mere settlement of a boundary dispute with a foreign country does not require an amendment;
it can be done by executive action.
8 · CITIZENSHIP
Constitutional provisions — Articles 5 to 11
Article 5 Citizenship at the commencement of the Constitution — a person domiciled in India who was born in India, or
either of whose parents was born in India, or who had been ordinarily resident in India for not less than five
years.
Article 6 Rights of citizenship of persons who migrated from Pakistan to India.
Article 7 Rights of citizenship of persons who migrated to Pakistan and later returned on a permit for resettlement.
Article 8 Rights of citizenship of persons of Indian origin residing outside India.
Article 9 A person voluntarily acquiring the citizenship of a foreign state ceases to be a citizen — no dual citizenship.
Article 10 Continuance of the rights of citizenship, subject to any law made by Parliament.
Article 11 Parliament to regulate the right of citizenship by law — under which the Citizenship Act, 1955 was
enacted.

Effects of a National Emergency
• Executive — the Centre may give executive directions to a state on any matter; the state government is not
suspended, but is brought under complete Central control.
• Legislative — Parliament may make laws on subjects in the State List. Such laws cease to operate six months after
the emergency ends. The state legislature is not suspended.
• Financial — the President may modify the distribution of revenues between the Centre and the states; such an
order operates till the end of the financial year in which the emergency ceases.
• Term of the legislature — the life of the Lok Sabha may be extended by one year at a time, not extending beyond
six months after the emergency ceases. The same applies to a state assembly.
• Article 358 — the six rights under Article 19 are automatically suspended. Since the 44th Amendment this
operates only when the emergency is declared on the ground of war or external aggression, not armed
rebellion, and only for laws related to the emergency.
• Article 359 — the President may by order suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of specified
Fundamental Rights. Articles 20 and 21 can never be suspended — a safeguard introduced by the 44th Amendment.
Effects of President's Rule
• The President assumes the functions of the state government and the powers of the Governor and of any state
authority other than the High Court.
• The state legislature is either suspended or dissolved; Parliament passes the state legislative bills and the state
budget.
• Parliament may delegate the law-making power for the state to the President or another authority.
• The President cannot assume the powers of the High Court or suspend any provision relating to it.

PROCEDURE — POINTS THAT ARE TESTED DIRECTLY
• An amendment bill may be introduced in either House, and by a private member as well as a minister; no prior
recommendation of the President is required.
• It must be passed separately in each House — there is no provision for a joint sitting in case of disagreement.
• Where state ratification is needed, the states must ratify before the bill is presented to the President; there is no time limit,
and a state cannot withdraw its ratification once given.
• The President must give assent — he can neither withhold assent nor return the bill for reconsideration.
• The Constitution can be amended only by Parliament; state legislatures cannot initiate an amendment, except a
resolution for the abolition or creation of a legislative council.

THE DISTINCTIONS MOST OFTEN CONFUSED
• Effective majority subtracts vacancies; absolute majority does not. A House of 245 with 5 vacancies needs 121 for an
effective majority and 123 for an absolute majority.
• National Emergency needs a special majority; President's Rule and a Financial Emergency need only a simple
majority.
• Removing a judge takes a special majority of the Article 368 type. Removing the Speaker takes only an effective majority.
Removing the President takes two-thirds of the total membership.
• Article 249 requires two-thirds of those present and voting only — it does not require a majority of the total membership.
12 · THE BASIC STRUCTURE DOCTRINE
• The question is how far Parliament's amending power under Article 368 extends. The Kesavananda Bharati case
(1973) settled it: Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution, including the Fundamental Rights, but
cannot alter its basic structure.
• The doctrine applies to amendments made on or after 24 April 1973, the date of that judgment.
• The Constitution nowhere defines the basic structure. It has been built up case by case, and the list is not closed.
Elements recognised as part of the basic structure
Supremacy of the Constitution · sovereign, democratic and republican nature of the polity · secular character · separation of
powers between the legislature, executive and judiciary · federal character · unity and integrity of the nation · welfare state and
the socio-economic justice objective · judicial review · independence of the judiciary · the rule of law · harmony and balance
between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles · free and fair elections · the principle of equality · limited power of
Parliament to amend the Constitution · effective access to justice · powers of the Supreme Court under Articles 32, 136,
141 and 142 · parliamentary system of government.
POINTS ON WHICH THE CURRENT POSITION DIFFERS FROM THE OLDER ONE
• The right to property is no longer a Fundamental Right. The 44th Amendment, 1978 removed Article 31 and made it a
legal right under Article 300A.
• The nomination of Anglo-Indian members to the Lok Sabha and state assemblies ceased with effect from 25 January
2020 under the 104th Amendment.
• The women's reservation introduced by the 106th Amendment, 2023 has been enacted but is not yet in operation — it
takes effect only after the next census and the delimitation that follows.
• Regarding cooperative societies: what stands firmly is the Fundamental Right to form them under Article 19(1)(c) and
the Directive Principle for their promotion under Article 43-B. Part IX-B was held inoperative for societies confined to a single
state, for want of ratification by the states, and survives only for multi-state societies.
• The safest figures to hold are the original ones — 395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules — since the present count of Articles is
stated variously depending on how inserted sub-articles are counted.
